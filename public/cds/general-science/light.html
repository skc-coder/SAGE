  • Concave Mirrors (Converging):

    • Headlights, Torches, & Searchlights: Bulb placed at focus produces a long-reaching, parallel beam.

    • Shaving & Dentist Mirrors: Placing objects very close (between P and F) gives an upright, zoomed-in (magnified) image.

    • Solar Furnaces: Focuses parallel sunlight onto a single point to generate intense heat.

  • Convex Mirrors (Diverging):

    • Rearview Mirrors: Curved outward to shrink objects down slightly, giving drivers an upright view over a much wider area (broader field of view).

TL;DR — Sign Convention

  • Origin: Pole (P) is (0,0). Light moves Left → Right.

  • Left / In front (−) | Right / Behind (+)

  • Above axis (+) | Below axis (−)

Quick Reference

  • u (Object distance): Always Negative (−)

  • f (Focal length):

    • Concave mirror: Negative (−)

    • Convex mirror: Positive (+)

  • v (Image distance):

    • Real image (in front): Negative (−)

    • Virtual image (behind): Positive (+)

  • m (Magnification =−uv​):

    • Negative (−) → Real & Inverted

    • Positive (+) → Virtual & Erect

Definition

The Absolute Refractive Index (n) of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum (c) to its speed in that medium (v):

n=vc​

  • Unitless & Dimensionless quantity.

  • Higher n = Optically denser medium = Light travels slower and bends more towards the normal.

Here is the breakdown of the Lens Formula, how it works, and key differences from the Mirror Formula based on your notes.

1. The Lens Formula

The Lens Formula relates object distance (u), image distance (v), and focal length (f) for thin spherical lenses:

v1​−u1​=f1​

  • u = Distance of the object from the optical center (O). Always negative (−) for real objects.

  • v = Distance of the image from the optical center (O).

    • Positive (+) if formed on the other side (Real Image).

    • Negative (−) if formed on the same side as object (Virtual Image).

  • f = Focal length of the lens.

    • Convex Lens: Always Positive (+)

    • Concave Lens: Always Negative (−)

2. Lens Formula vs. Mirror Formula

The crucial difference stems from how light behaves: Mirrors reflect light back to the same side, while Lenses refract light through to the other side.

3. Power of Lens (P) & Combination Formulas

As highlighted in your notes:

  1. Power Formula: Power is the reciprocal of focal length:

    P=f (in meters)1​orP=f (in cm)100​

    • Unit: Dioptre (D) or m−1.

    • Convex Lens: f>0⟹P>0 (Positive Power).

    • Concave Lens: f<0⟹P<0 (Negative Power).

  2. Combination of Lenses in Contact:

    • Total Power (P): Powers add up algebraically:

      P=P1​+P2​+P3​+…

    • Total Magnification (m): Magnifications multiply together:

      m=m1​×m2​×m3​×…

4. Easy Trick to Remember the Sign Difference

  • Lens Formula Has a Minus Sign (−): Think of Lens = Less (Subtraction →v1​−u1​=f1​).

  • Mirror Formula Has a Plus Sign (+): Think of Mirror = More (Addition →v1​+u1​=f1​).

  • Magnification flips the sign:

    • Lens formula has −, so Lens m gets + (m=uv​).

    • Mirror formula has +, so Mirror m gets − (m=−uv​).

The sign of m MUST match the orientation of the image:

  • Negative m = Inverted (Real)

  • Positive m = Upright (Virtual)

Because mirror real images form on the same side (v and u share the same sign), we must add a − to force m to be negative. Because lens real images form on opposite sides (v and u have opposite signs), the ratio uv​ handles it automatically

TL;DR — Optical Instruments Summary

  • Periscope:

    • Setup: 2 plane mirrors parallel to each other at 45∘.

    • Principle: Uses the Law of Reflection to turn light rays twice (90∘ each) to see over obstacles.

  • Compound Microscope:

    • Setup: 2 Convex lenses (Objective + Eyepiece).

    • Principle: Objective creates a magnified intermediate real image; eyepiece magnifies it further for viewing tiny, close objects.

  • Astronomical Telescope:

    • Setup: 2 Convex lenses (Objective with large focal length f↑ + Eyepiece).

    • Principle: Large objective gathers maximum light from far-away objects (stars/planets) to create a focal image, which the eyepiece magnifies (image is inverted).

  • Terrestrial Telescope:

    • Setup: 3 Convex lenses (Objective + Erecting Lens + Eyepiece).

    • Principle: The middle erecting lens flips the upside-down image right-side up without changing its size so land objects can be viewed normally. eyes prims

Mirrors always follow f=R/2 (2f=R), whereas lenses rely on the Lens Maker’s Formula—f1​=(n−1)(R1​1​−R2​1​)—to account for both surfaces and the refractive index. Because of this, 2f only equals R under specific symmetric conditions, and applying proper sign conventions ensures lenses like biconcave ones correctly yield a negative focal length.

TL;DR Answer:

c) The image will be formed at infinity

(Placing an object between F and P creates a virtual, erect, and magnified image behind the mirror. An image is formed at infinity only when the object is placed directly at focus F.)

Which ray diagram is correct

The correct option is c) 1 and 4 only.

Explanation:

The defect described in the question—where a person can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects clearly—is Myopia (nearsightedness).

In a myopic eye, rays of light coming from distant objects focus in front of the retina instead of directly on it.

Evaluating the Statements:

  1. Excessive curvature of the eye lens (Correct):

    • Increased curvature makes the lens thicker and increases its converging power, causing distant light rays to bend too sharply and focus in front of the retina.

  2. The eyeball has become too small (Incorrect):

    • If the eyeball is too small (short), the image forms behind the retina. This causes Hypermetropia (farsightedness), not myopia.

  3. The focal length of the eye lens is too large (Incorrect):

    • A larger focal length means less converging power, which also causes hypermetropia. In myopia, the focal length of the eye lens is actually too short.

  4. Elongation of the eyeball (Correct):

    • If the eyeball is longer than normal along its optical axis, the distance between the eye lens and the retina increases, so the image forms in front of the retina.

Summary of Causes for Myopia:

  • Excessive curvature of the eye lens (Statement 1)

  • Elongation of the eyeball (Statement 4)

In farsightedness (Hypermetropia), the situation is the exact opposite of myopia. A person can see distant objects clearly, but nearby objects appear blurry.

Here is how the anatomy works in hypermetropia:

Causes of Farsightedness

  1. Shortening of the Eyeball:

    • The front-to-back distance of the eyeball is too short (decreased internal distance between the lens and retina).

    • Because the retina sits closer to the lens than normal, light rays from nearby objects don’t have enough room to converge in time—they attempt to form a focus behind the retina.

  2. Insufficient Curvature / Too Large Focal Length:

    • The eye lens is too flat (not curved enough) or lacks sufficient bending power.

    • A flatter lens has a longer focal length, meaning it cannot bend light sharply enough to focus it on the screen (retina) in front of it.
