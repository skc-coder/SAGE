Money — Evolution, Types & Supply

Topic: Economy > Monetary Economics > Money Source: CDS Foundation 2026 — Money 01 & 02 Class Notes + DPP 01, DPP 02 (PW/Defence Wallah) Tags: economy money banking cds2026 Links: cds/humanities/Banking — History, Structure & Regulation | cds/humanities/Economy MOC

Overview

Traces money from barter to CBDC, covers legal tender classification, crypto currency mechanics, and closes with money supply (M0–M4) and the money multiplier — the two most numerically-tested ideas in this chapter.

1. Evolution of Money

Money didn’t appear fully formed — it evolved to solve a specific coordination failure in trade. Each stage below solves a problem the previous stage created.

1.1 Barter System

The barter system is a system of exchange where goods are exchanged directly for goods, with no intermediary medium.

Its core failure is the double coincidence of wants — for a trade to happen, each party must want exactly what the other is offering, at the same time. An ice-cream seller wanting wheat must find a farmer who specifically wants ice cream right now. This friction is what every later stage of money exists to remove.

1.2 Commodity Money

When a particular commodity is given the status of a medium of exchange, that commodity becomes money. Historical examples include cowrie shells (Kori), tobacco, cocoa, cattle, and animal hides/horns.

Key Idea

Commodity money works because the commodity itself has independent, widely-recognized value — you’d want it even if it stopped being “money.”

1.3 Functions of Money

Function TierIncludes
PrimaryMedium of exchange, Measure of value
SecondaryStore of value, Standard of deferred payment
TertiaryBuying luxury/discretionary goods

1.4 Metallic Currency

A particular piece of metal is given status as a medium of exchange. Early forms had no fixed dimension (punch-marked coins); later coinage standardized shape and weight.

Full-bodied coins: a coin’s intrinsic value (value of the metal itself) is greater than its face value (value printed/stamped on it).

Token coins: the reverse — intrinsic value is less than face value. Modern Indian coins (₹1, ₹2, ₹5) fell into this category, and their size/weight was reduced by the RBI over time, with older, heavier coin series withdrawn from circulation.

Full-bodied coin: Vintrinsic​>Vface​Token coin: Vintrinsic​<Vface​

1.5 Fiat Currency

Currency issued by the government, given legal tender status to settle payments and transactions — its value rests entirely on government backing/law, not on any commodity behind it.

Commemorative currency is a special case: currency issued by the government to mark or celebrate a historical event or person, rather than for general circulation.

1.6 Legal Tender Classification

TypeDefinitionExample
Limited Legal TenderCan be used only up to a fixed number/value in a single transaction50p, ₹1 coins
Unlimited Legal TenderCan be used in any quantity to settle any transaction₹500, ₹200, ₹100, ₹50, ₹20, ₹10 notes

1.7 Fiduciary Currency

Currency issued by an organization (not the government) to settle payments, accepted purely because people trust the issuer. Examples: cheques, demand drafts.

Key Idea

A cheque is fiduciary money, not fiat money — it isn’t legal tender; it works only because the payee trusts the issuing bank will honor it. This distinction is a favorite trap in MCQs (see DPP Q3, Q6 below).

1.8 Demonetization

Demonetization is when the government withdraws the legal tender status of a currency unit. Common reasons: curbing fake currency, fighting corruption, formalizing the economy.

India’s demonetization years: 1946, 1978, 2016.

1.9 Plastic Currency & Cryptocurrency

Plastic currency refers to debit/credit cards.

Cryptocurrency / virtual currency is a value generated by a computer program by solving an algorithm; when that generated value is used by people as a medium of exchange, it becomes a crypto currency. Examples: Bitcoin, Litecoin, Ethereum, Ripple, Dogecoin, Libra Coin.

  • Satoshi Nakamoto — creator of Bitcoin.
  • Runs on blockchain technology — a decentralised ledger system.
  • Mining: solving a computational puzzle to validate transactions and earn a reward.
MeritsDemerits
Anonymity — parties to a transaction stay hiddenNo physical backing
Lower transaction costCan be used in terror financing, human trafficking
Lower transaction timePrice volatility
Seen as a candidate future currency

Stablecoins: a crypto currency whose price is pegged to a real-world commodity or currency (e.g., gold, USD, EUR) to reduce volatility.

Regulatory Note

Private cryptocurrency is not legal tender in India. This is distinct from the RBI’s own digital currency initiative below.

1.10 Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC)

CBDC is a fiat currency issued by the RBI in digital form — the digital rupee (e₹). It:

  • Is legal tender.
  • Sits on the liability side of the RBI’s balance sheet (just like physical currency in circulation).
  • Uses blockchain-adjacent distributed ledger technology.
  • Flows: RBI → Commercial banks → Retail/Wholesale users.

1.11 CBDC vs UPI

FeatureCBDC (Digital Rupee)UPI
NatureDigital legal tender, like physical cashRail for moving bank deposits
IssuerReserve Bank of IndiaNPCI (with banks as participants)
SettlementFinal at central bank levelInterbank via NPCI
Offline capabilityPossible (tested in pilots)Needs internet/mobile network
AnonymityCan be designed for cash-like privacyFully linked to KYC’d bank accounts
Use casesOffline payments, cross-border, programmable money, cash replacementEveryday P2P and merchant payments

Key Idea

UPI never creates new money — it just moves existing bank deposits faster. CBDC is a genuinely new form of central-bank money, parallel to cash.

2. Money Supply & Money Multiplier

2.1 Money Creation (Credit Multiplication)

When a deposit enters the banking system, only a fraction (the Legal Reserve Ratio) is held back; the rest is lent out, redeposited elsewhere, and re-lent — each round shrinking geometrically.

Worked example from the notes (Legal Reserve Ratio = 10%):

X depositsSBI reserves 10%Aman deposits 900 in HDFC, HDFC reserves 90, lends 810 to ShriShri deposits 810 in Yes Bank, reserves 81, lends 729 to RamRam deposits 729 in PNB​=1000 in SBI=100, lends 900 to Aman

Total new deposits generated so far: 1000+900+810+729=3439, and the chain continues asymptotically.

2.2 Money Multiplier Formula

The money multiplier measures how much total money supply an initial deposit can generate in the economy.

m=Legal Reserve Ratio1​

With a 10% reserve ratio:

m=0.101​=10

So every ₹1 of fresh deposit can theoretically expand to ₹10 of money supply. In practice, credit creation is limited by:

  • Banking habits of the population (how much cash people keep vs. deposit)
  • The legal reserve ratio itself
  • Availability of cash in the system
  • Demand for loans
  • Prevailing economic conditions

Key Idea

A higher reserve ratio shrinks the multiplier; a population with strong banking habits (depositing rather than hoarding cash) raises it. This is why DPP 02 Q7 answers “increase in banking habits” as the driver of a higher multiplier, not the reserve ratio.

2.3 Liquidity Ranking

Liquidity = how easily an asset converts into cash.

Cash>Bank Deposit>Gold>Land(most→least liquid)

Within deposits: Demand Deposits (savings/current accounts, withdrawable on demand) are more liquid than Time Deposits (fixed/recurring deposits, locked for a term).

2.4 Money Supply Aggregates (M0–M4)

Ranked top-to-bottom by liquidity (highest to lowest); amount increases down the pyramid while liquidity decreases.

AggregateCompositionClassification
M0​Currency + coins with public + Bankers’ deposits with RBI + Other deposits with RBI (= High-Powered/Base Money)Most liquid
M1​Currency with public (CC) + Net Demand Deposits (DD) + Other deposits with RBI (OD)Narrow Money
M2​M1​ + Savings deposits with Post OfficeNarrow Money
M3​M1​ + Time deposits with the banking systemBroad Money
M4​M3​ + All Post Office deposits (excluding National Savings Certificates)Broad Money, least liquid
M1​=CC+DD+OD

Key Idea

M3​ is the most widely tracked/used measure of money supply in India’s monetary policy framework (this is what DPP 02 Q10 is testing).

3. PYQ / DPP Practice

Money DPP 01 — Q1

Question: Which of the following is associated with the introduction of token money?

Approach: Recall the definition — token money is where face value exceeds intrinsic (metal) value.

Answer: B — Value of money is greater than its intrinsic value​

Money DPP 01 — Q2

Question: Chronological order of evolution of money.

Approach: Barter → Commodity → Metallic → Paper → Digital is the standard progression; among the given options this maps to Commodity → Metallic → Paper → Digital.

Answer: B​

Money DPP 01 — Q3

Question: Which are examples of fiduciary money — Cheques, Coins, Currency notes, Demand deposits?

Approach: Fiduciary money rests on trust in the issuer rather than legal tender status or intrinsic value — this fits cheques and demand deposits, not coins/notes (which are fiat/token money).

Answer: A — I and IV only​

Money DPP 01 — Q4

Question: Example of “near money.”

Answer: B — Savings deposits​

Money DPP 01 — Q5

Question: Which are primarily associated with digital/cashless transactions — bank deposits, credit cards, debit cards, electronic money?

Answer: A — b, c and d​

Money DPP 01 — Q6

Question: Match Token Money / Fiduciary Money / Fiat Money to their descriptions.

Approach: Token = face value > intrinsic value by custom/law (a); Fiduciary = trust-based medium (b); Fiat = government-issued legal tender, not commodity-backed (c).

Answer: C — 1-a, 2-b, 3-c​

Money DPP 01 — Q7

Question: Statements on CBDC — (1) digital fiat issued by RBI, (2) decentralised like Bitcoin, (3) pegged to domestic currency & legal tender.

Approach: CBDC is centrally issued and controlled by RBI — statement 2 is false since it is not decentralised like Bitcoin. Statements 1 and 3 hold.

Answer: C — 1 and 3 only​

Money DPP 01 — Q8

Question: Key difference between CBDC and Bitcoin-like cryptocurrency.

Answer: D — CBDC is legal tender issued by the central bank, cryptocurrency is not​

Money DPP 01 — Q9

Question: What does fiat money indicate?

Answer: A​

Money DPP 01 — Q10

Question: Defining characteristic of commodity money.

Answer: A — Backed by a physical commodity or valuable resource​

Money DPP 02 — Q1

Question: M3​ and M4​ form which type of money?

Answer: B — Broad Money​

Money DPP 02 — Q2

Question: Correct statement on liquidity of money supply measures in India.

Approach: M1​ most liquid, M4​ least liquid — the pyramid ordering.

Answer: D — M1​ is the most liquid, M4​ is the least liquid​

Money DPP 02 — Q3

Question: What does “money supply” refer to?

Answer: C — Total quantity of money held by the public in various forms​

Money DPP 02 — Q4

Question: What does High-Powered Money (H) consist of?

Answer: C — Currency held by the public and cash reserves with banks​

Money DPP 02 — Q5

Question: Which measure increases aggregate money supply?

Approach: Central government borrowing from RBI for deficit financing is essentially “printing money” — it directly expands M0​/base money, unlike the other options which are neutral or contractionary.

Answer: D​

Money DPP 02 — Q6

Question: What’s included in M2​ but not M1​?

Answer: B — Post Office savings deposits​

Money DPP 02 — Q7

Question: The money multiplier increases with which factor?

Approach: Directly ties to the multiplier’s dependency list — banking habits raise deposit retention, which raises the multiplier’s real-world effect.

Answer: B — Increase in banking habits of the population​

Money DPP 02 — Q8

Question: Transaction Money is also known as?

Answer: D — M1​​

Money DPP 02 — Q9

Question: Statements on the Multiplier Effect.

Approach: Statement 1 (proportional income change from a spending injection/withdrawal) is correct; statement 2 is false — the multiplier is directly, not inversely, related to the marginal propensity to consume.

Answer: A — 1 only​

Money DPP 02 — Q10

Question: Most widely used form of money supply in the Indian monetary system.

Answer: C — M3​​
