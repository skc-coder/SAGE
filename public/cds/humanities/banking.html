Banking — History, Structure & Regulation

Topic: Economy > Monetary Economics > Banking Source: CDS Foundation 2026 — Banking 01, 02, 03 Class Notes + DPP 01, DPP 02, DPP 03 (PW/Defence Wallah) Tags: economy banking rbi cds2026 Links: Money — Evolution, Types & Supply | cds/humanities/Economy MOC

Overview

Covers the full arc from pre-colonial banking through nationalisation, mergers, and modern classification, then moves into NBFCs, differentiated banks, priority sector lending, interest-rate benchmarks, and the Basel risk framework.

1. History of Indian Banking

1.1 What Is a Bank

A simple framing: a bank is a trusted intermediary that helps manage money across three functions — saving (safekeeping deposits), borrowing (lending for big purchases), and growing (paying interest on savings).

More formally, banks are financial intermediaries sitting between households and business houses, channeling funds from savers to borrowers. The word “bank” traces etymologically through Greek civilization → Italian “banca” → the moneylender’s bench.

1.2 Colonial-Era Banking Timeline

YearEvent
1770Bank of Hindustan established — India’s first bank, HQ Calcutta (linked to early Portuguese/English trading company presence)
1806Bank of Calcutta founded
1809Renamed Bank of Bengal
1840Bank of Bombay established
1843Bank of Madras established
1881Oudh Commercial Bank — first commercial bank
1894Punjab National Bank, Lahore — first fully Indian-owned bank
1911Central Bank of India — first fully “swadeshi” (indigenous) bank
1921The three Presidency Banks (Bengal, Bombay, Madras) merged into the Imperial Bank of India

Commercial banks are those that deal directly with the common person, distinguishing them from purely central-banking functions.

1.3 Birth of the RBI

  • 1926: Hilton-Young Commission (Royal Commission on Currency and Finance) recommended establishing a central bank for India.
  • Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934 passed.
  • 1 April 1935: RBI commenced operations. First Governor: Osborne Smith.
  • 1947–1949: The Government of India nationalised the RBI.
  • RBI’s jurisdiction historically extended to India, Pakistan, and Myanmar.
  • 1949: Banking Regulation Act enacted.

Key Idea

The RBI’s founding sits between the policy recommendation (Hilton-Young, 1926) and the legal instrument (RBI Act, 1934) and operational start (1935) — DPP questions frequently test which of these three dates corresponds to which event (see Banking DPP 01, Q2).

1.4 Functions of the RBI

FunctionDescription
Monetary authorityControls money supply, implements monetary policy
Issuer of currencySole authority to issue currency notes; distributes and destroys unfit notes (note: ₹1 notes and 50p coins bear the Finance Secretary’s signature, not the RBI Governor’s)
Banker to governmentProvides Ways and Means Advances (WMA) — a 90-day short-term overdraft facility to Union/State governments. Two types: with collateral, and without collateral

1.5 Forex Reserve Composition

The RBI is the custodian of India’s forex reserves. China holds the world’s largest forex reserves; India became the 4th country to cross the $700 billion forex reserve mark.

Components (pyramid, largest to smallest role):

  • Gold
  • Foreign Currency Assets
  • SDR — Special Drawing Rights
  • RT — Reserve Tranche

SDR (Special Drawing Rights):

  • An international reserve asset created by the IMF in 1969.
  • Known as “Paper Gold” or the “Artificial Currency of the IMF.”
  • Is neither a currency nor a direct claim on the IMF.
  • Its value basket includes five currencies: US Dollar, Japanese Yen, Pound Sterling, Euro, Chinese Renminbi.

2. Bank Mergers & Restructuring

The Narasimhan Committee recommended merging large, profitable banks into fewer, stronger institutions — the “too big to fail” doctrine, intended to help Indian banks compete globally and comply with Basel norms.

Trigger conditions cited:

  • PSBs competing with each other, eroding profits.
  • Rising Non-Performing Assets (NPA).
  • Recurring burden on government to recapitalize PSBs.
  • No Indian bank competitive at the international level.

Non-Performing Asset (NPA): a loan where principal and/or interest hasn’t been repaid within the due period (standard reference: 90 days past due).

3. Bank Classification & NBFCs

3.1 Financial Intermediaries Landscape

Financial Intermediaries=Banks (e.g., SBI, HDFC, PNB)+NBFCs (e.g., Manappuram, Muthoot)

3.2 Banks vs NBFCs

FeatureBanksNBFCs
Governing lawBanking Regulation Act, 1949 & RBI Act, 1934Companies Act, 1956/2013; regulated by RBI
Demand deposits (CASA)Can acceptCannot accept — only term/time deposits
Cheque issuanceCan issue cheques drawn on themselvesCannot issue cheques on themselves
Payment & Settlement SystemPart of itNot part of it
DICGC insuranceDeposits insured up to \text{Rs. }5 lakhNot insured
Foreign investmentUp to 74% (private sector banks)Up to 100%

Key Idea

The single most-tested NBFC fact: NBFCs cannot accept demand deposits and cannot issue their own cheques — this distinction alone answers most DPP questions on the topic (see DPP 02 Q6, Q7; DPP 03 Q1).

3.3 Scheduled vs Non-Scheduled Banks

Classification is based on the 2nd Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934.

  • Scheduled Bank: listed in the 2nd Schedule; paid-up capital + reserves ≥ \text{Rs. }5 lakh.
  • Non-Scheduled Bank: not listed; capital < \text{Rs. }5 lakh (very few remain today).

3.4 Full Classification of Banks in India

A. Scheduled Banks

  • I. Commercial Banks
    1. Public Sector Banks (PSBs) — Govt. stake ≥ 51%. E.g., SBI, PNB
    2. Private Sector Banks — private ownership. E.g., HDFC, ICICI
    3. Foreign Banks — HQ abroad, branches in India. E.g., Citibank, HSBC
    4. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) — sponsored by PSBs, rural credit focus. E.g., Prathama Bank
  • II. Cooperative Banks — “no profit, no loss” principle, serve members (Urban & Rural)

B. Non-Scheduled Banks — capital < \text{Rs. }5 lakh

C. Other Categories

  1. Payments Banks — no lending, deposits capped at \text{Rs. }2 lakh. E.g., Paytm Payments Bank
  2. Small Finance Banks (SFBs) — niche banking for unserved segments. E.g., AU Small Finance Bank

Commercial vs Non-commercial: Commercial banks deal directly with the general public; non-commercial banks (e.g., NABARD, RBI) do not.

Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D-SIBs): currently identified by RBI as SBI, HDFC, ICICI — institutions whose failure would have outsized systemic impact.

3.5 Cooperative Banks

A cooperative bank is a financial institution registered with the Registrar of Cooperatives, where the owners and customers are the same people. Supervised by NABARD, but regulated by the RBI.

Structure:

  • Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs)
  • Rural — split further into Long-term and Short-term, across three tiers:
    • State level → State Cooperative Bank
    • District level → Central Cooperative Bank
    • Village level → Primary Agriculture Cooperative Societies (PACS) — the direct interface with farmers.

3.6 All India Financial Institutions (AIFIs)

InstitutionFull Form / Role
RBICentral bank
NHBNational Housing Bank (under RBI)
NABARDNational Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (est. 12 July 1982, on the recommendation of the Shivaraman Committee)
SIDBISmall Industries Development Bank of India
EXIM BankExport-Import Bank
NaBFIDNational Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development — announced as a DFI in Budget 2021

3.7 Development Financial Institutions (DFIs)

DFIs support developmental, capital-intensive projects (infrastructure etc.). They:

  • Do not accept public deposits.
  • Raise funds via borrowing/debt from government, insurance companies, etc.
  • Provide guarantees to banks on behalf of customers.
  • Offer technical assistance to companies.

India’s first DFI: Industrial Finance Corporation of India (IFCI), 1948.

DFI CategoryInstitutions
All-India levelNABARD, SIDBI, NHB, EXIM Bank, NaBFID (2021)
Sector-specificPFC (Power Finance Corporation), REC (Rural Electrification Corporation), IRFC (Indian Railway Finance Corporation)

4. Differentiated Banks & Financial Inclusion

Differentiated banks are institutions that differ from standard commercial banks in their area of work, structure, or function.

4.1 Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) — 1975

Ownership structure: 50% Union Government, 15% State Government, 35% Sponsor Bank.

First RRB: Prathama Bank, Moradabad, Uttar Pradesh.

4.2 Priority Sector Lending (PSL) — 1972

Norms requiring banks to direct a fixed percentage of total credit toward designated priority sectors.

Bank TypePSL Target
Commercial Banks40% total (Agriculture 18%, Micro enterprises 7%, Weaker sections 12%)
RRBs75%
Urban Cooperative Banks (UCB)60%

Priority sectors: Agriculture, Micro enterprises, Weaker sections, Entrepreneurship, Rural infrastructure, Health, Education.

Shortfall mechanisms: banks failing PSL targets must route the deficit through the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF) or Small Industries Development Fund (SIDF).

PSL Certificates (introduced 2016): tradeable instruments letting a bank that overachieved its PSL target sell its surplus to a bank that fell short — allowing the shortfall bank to “buy” compliance.

4.3 Financial Inclusion

The principle that every person should have access and opportunity to avail financial services.

Key initiatives: Bank Mitra, Jan Dhan Yojana (No-Frills Accounts with overdraft facility), PMSBY, PMJJBY, APS.

Nachiket Mor Committee — recommended niche/differentiated banking; its recommendations led directly to Payments Banks (2014) and Small Finance Banks (2016).

4.4 Payments Banks (2014)

  • Cannot provide loans or issue credit cards.
  • Can accept deposits, but capped at \text{Rs. }2 lakh per account.
  • Cannot accept NRI deposits.

4.5 Small Finance Banks (2016)

  • At least 25% of branches must be in rural areas.
  • 75% of total credit must go to priority sector.
  • 50% of loans must go to Micro, Small & Medium Enterprises (MSMEs).
  • 50% of total credit must consist of loans below \text{Rs. }25 lakh.

5. Interest Rate Benchmarks

Banks have used successive frameworks to set the interest rates they charge customers:

BenchmarkDescription
Prime Lending RateLower interest rate offered to a bank’s most favored customers
Base RateMinimum interest rate a bank can charge any customer
MCLR (2016)Marginal Cost of Lending Rate
EBLR (2019)External Benchmark Lending Rate — replaced MCLR
MCLR=Marginal cost of funds+Negative carry on CRR+Operating cost+Tenor premium

EBLR ties lending rates to external, market-observable benchmarks rather than internal bank costs: (i) Repo Rate — rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks, generally overnight/short-term (ii) 91-day Treasury Bill rate (iii) 182-day Treasury Bill rate (iv) Rate published by Financial Benchmark India Ltd.

6. Basel Norms & Bank Risk Management

6.1 Basel Framework Timeline

Basel, Switzerland, 1974 — the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision was formed by the Group of 10 (G-10) countries; it now has 48 member countries. Basel norms exist to prevent bank runs and strengthen banking infrastructure.

Basel VersionYearFocus
Basel I1988Credit risk only; CRAR (Capital to Risk-weighted Assets Ratio) set at 8%
Basel II2004Three pillars — Capital Adequacy, Supervisory Review, Market Discipline
Basel III2010 (India: 2019)CRAR raised to 12.9% (Tier-I 10.5%, Tier-II 2%); Counter-Cyclical Buffer 0–2.5%; Liquidity Coverage Ratio (30-day highly liquid asset requirement)
CRAR=Risk-Weighted AssetsCapital​×100≥8% (Basel I minimum)

Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR): requires banks to fund their activities using stable sources of finance over a longer horizon.

Tier structure: Tier-I capital = equity; Tier-II capital = subordinated debt/loan-type instruments.

Worked CRAR example from notes:

Collateralised loan: Uncollateralised loan (higher risk weight): Total Risk-Weighted AssetsRequired reserve (10%)​100×1=100500×10=5000=5100=510→this is the CRAR-mandated buffer​

6.2 Categories of Bank Risk

Risk TypeDescription
Credit RiskBorrower defaults on loan/interest → leads to NPAs
Market RiskLoss from price movements — Interest Rate Risk (bond prices fall as rates rise), Forex Risk (currency fluctuation), Equity Risk
Operational RiskLoss from failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events — fraud, cyberattacks, system failure, human error
Liquidity RiskInability to meet short-term cash obligations without unacceptable loss; can trigger a bank run; caused by asset-liability mismatch
Reputational RiskLoss from damage to brand/image — scandals, poor service
OtherStrategic Risk, Compliance/Legal Risk, Cyber Risk (a subset of Operational Risk)

Key Idea

Capital Adequacy is fundamentally about holding a buffer proportional to risk-weighted, not raw, assets — an uncollateralised loan of the same face value contributes far more to required reserves than a collateralised one, exactly as in the worked example above.

Question: Differentiated Banking — SFBs/Payments Banks recommended by Nachiket Mor Committee; Payments Banks can issue credit cards and accept time deposits; FDI not allowed in Payments Banks. Which are incorrect?

Approach: Statement 1 correct. Statement 2 incorrect — Payments Banks cannot issue credit cards nor accept time deposits (only demand deposits up to \text{Rs. }2 lakh). Statement 3 incorrect — FDI is permitted in Payments Banks.

Approach: All three match Sections 3.3 and 4.1 exactly.

Answer: D — 1, 2, and 3​

Question: NBFCs can access RBI’s Liquidity Adjustment Facility; FIIs can hold G-Secs; Stock exchanges can offer separate debt trading platforms.

Answer: D — 2 and 3 only​

Banking DPP 03 — Q9

Question: PACS — directly regulated by RBI; final link between borrowers and higher financing agencies; farmers get crop loans at 2% via subsidy.

Approach: PACS are supervised by NABARD, not directly regulated by RBI — statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is correct (matches the three-tier cooperative structure in Section 3.5). Statement 3’s specific 2% figure isn’t substantiated in these notes and should be treated cautiously.

Banking DPP 03 — Q10
