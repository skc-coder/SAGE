Sample Space, Events, Axioms and theorems of Probability

Topic: GATE CS > Probability > Foundations Source: GO Classes — Probability Lec (Reference: John Tsitsiklis, MIT 6.041 course notes) Tags: probability axioms foundations gate2027 Links: mocs/moc probablity | Inclusion-Exclusion & De Morgan’s Law for Probability

Overview This note covers the building blocks of probability theory: experiments, sample spaces, events, the probability function, and the three axioms that everything else in the course is built on.

1. Scope of the Course

1.1 What will be studied

The course builds probability from first principles: basic definitions, sample space and events, Bayes’ theorem, and random variables/probability distributions.

1.2 What will NOT be studied

Combinatorics-style counting (permutations & combinations) is treated as a separate topic under Discrete Mathematics, not under Probability.

Reference Primary reference text: John Tsitsiklis' MIT probability course notes.

2. What Does “Probability” Actually Mean?

2.1 The frequentist intuition

Saying “probability of heads on a fair coin is 1/2” does not mean that in any small number of tosses you’ll get exactly half heads.

  • Toss 2 times → 1 head? Not guaranteed.
  • Toss 4 times → 2 heads? Not guaranteed.
  • Toss 100 times → 50 heads? Not guaranteed.

Even 100 heads in a row is possible, just extremely unlikely: probability (1/2)100.

2.2 The long-run limit

As the number of trials grows very large, the fraction of heads converges to 1/2. Karl Pearson tossed a coin 24,000 times and got 12,012 heads; a separate experiment of 2,000 tosses gave 996 heads — both very close to 50%.

limn→∞​nn(H)​=21​

where n(H) is the number of heads observed and n is the total number of tosses.

Key Idea Probability describes long-run relative frequency, not a guarantee about any specific finite run.

3. Sample Space and Events

3.1 Experiment and Sample Space

An experiment is an underlying process that produces exactly one out of several possible outcomes. The sample space Ω (or S) is the set of all possible outcomes of that experiment.

Example — tossing a coin: Ω=H,T.

3.2 Properties a valid sample space must satisfy

  • It is a list (set) of possible outcomes.
  • The outcomes must be mutually exclusive (no overlap).
  • The outcomes must be collectively exhaustive (cover every possibility).

3.3 Events

An event is a subset of the sample space — a collection of possible outcomes.

Example — rolling a die, sample space Ω=1,2,3,4,5,6:

  • E1​=1,2, P(E1​)=2/6
  • E2​=3,4,5, P(E2​)=3/6
  • E3​=5, P(E3​)=1/6 (intersection region of E1​,E2​-style diagram in lecture)

The set of all possible events is the power set 2Ω. For Ω=H,T:

2^{\Omega} = \big{, \varnothing,\ {H},\ {T},\ {H,T} ,\big}

with P(∅)=0, P(H)=1/2, P(T)=1/2, P(H,T)=1.

3.4 Probability as a function

Probability is a function that maps every event (a subset of Ω) to a real number:

P:2Ω⟶[0,1]

Key Idea Ω is the universe of outcomes; an event is a subset; probability is a function from subsets to [0,1]. Keep these three roles distinct.

3.5 Worked sample space examples

ExperimentSample space
Sex of a newborn childS=g,b
Finish order of a 7-horse race (post positions 1–7)S = {all 7! permutations of (1,2,3,4,5,6,7)}
Flipping two coinsS=(H,H),(H,T),(T,H),(T,T)
Tossing a coin twiceHH,HT,TH,TT
Rolling a dieΩ=1,2,3,4,5,6

For the horse race: knowing only who finished first gives sample space 1,…,7 (7 outcomes). Knowing the first two finishing positions gives 7×6=42 ordered outcomes, e.g. 12,13,14,…,76.

4. Axioms of Probability

4.1 The three axioms

1. Nonnegativity:​P(A)≥0 2. Normalization:​P(Ω)=1 3. Additivity:​If A∩B=∅, then P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)​

For a partition of the sample space into disjoint elementary outcomes s1​,s2​,s3​:

P(s1​,s2​,s3​)=P(s1​)+P(s2​)+P(s3​)=1

More generally, for disjoint outcomes s1​,…,sn​ covering Ω:

P(s1​,s2​,…,sn​)=P(s1​)+P(s2​)+⋯+P(sn​)

4.2 Countable Additivity Axiom

If A1​,A2​,… are pairwise disjoint events (possibly infinitely many, but countable), then:

P(A1​∪A2​∪⋯)=P(A1​)+P(A2​)+⋯

Countable vs Uncountable This axiom only applies to a countable union. For an uncountable union (e.g., summing probability over every real point in [0,1]), the additivity argument breaks down — that's why a single point on a continuous interval, e.g. P(X=0.2), is 0 even though the interval itself has probability 1.

5. Equally Likely Outcomes

5.1 Deriving P(si​)=1/n

If all outcomes s1​,…,sn​ are equally likely, Axiom 2 (normalization) plus Axiom 3 (additivity) give:

P(s1​∪s2​∪⋯∪sn​)​=1 P(s1​)+P(s2​)+⋯+P(sn​)​=1 ⇒P(si​)​=n1​for every i​

5.2 Probability of an event under equally likely outcomes

For an event A=s1​,…,sk​:

P(A)=k termsn1​+n1​+⋯+n1​​​=nk​=total number of sample pointsnumber of elements in A​

Common Mistake

This formula (count of favorable / total count) only works when outcomes are equally likely. The lecture explicitly shows a counter-example where s1​,s2​,s3​ have probabilities 1/6,1/6,2/6 (not equal) — here P(s1​,s2​,s3​)=4/6=2/3, not 3/6 (which you’d wrongly get by just counting elements).

6. Worked PYQ

Two Dice — Sum Equals 7 Question: If two dice are rolled, what is the probability that the sum of the upturned faces equals 7?

Approach: Step 1 — Total equally likely outcomes when rolling two dice: 6×6=36. Step 2 — Favorable outcomes summing to 7: (1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1) — 6 outcomes.

P(sum=7)=nn(A)​=366​=61​

Answer: 1/6​

Inclusion-Exclusion & De Morgan’s Law for Probability

Topic: GATE CS > Probability > Foundations Source: GO Classes — Probability Lec Tags: probability inclusion-exclusion demorgan gate2027 Links: mocs/moc probablity | gate-cs/math/Probability foundations

Overview Covers the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle (2 and 3 events, with proof) and De Morgan's Laws applied to probability, plus two classic GATE PYQs that use them directly.

1. Inclusion-Exclusion Principle

1.1 Two events

P(E∪F)=P(E)+P(F)−P(E∩F)

1.2 Three events

P(E∪F∪G)= ​P(E)+P(F)+P(G) ​−P(E∩F)−P(E∩G)−P(F∩G) ​+P(E∩F∩G)​

1.3 Proof for two events (via disjoint decomposition)

Split the Venn diagram of E and F into three disjoint regions:

  • a=E∩Fc (only E)
  • b=E∩F (overlap)
  • c=F∩Ec (only F)

Since E∪F=a∪b∪c and these three pieces are disjoint, Axiom 3 gives:

P(E∪F)=P(a)+P(b)+P(c)

Now express P(E), P(F), and P(E∩F) in terms of a,b,c:

P(E)​=P(a)+P(b) P(F)​=P(b)+P(c) P(E∩F)​=P(b)​

So:

P(E)+P(F)−P(E∩F)=[P(a)+P(b)]+[P(b)+P(c)]−P(b)=P(a)+P(b)+P(c)

which matches P(E∪F) exactly — the extra P(b) term cancels because b=E∩F was double-counted once in P(E) and once in P(F).

Key Idea Inclusion-Exclusion is really just "count the overlap once, not twice" — the proof works by breaking the union into disjoint pieces where plain additivity (Axiom 3) applies directly.

2. De Morgan’s Law for Probability

(E∩F)c=Ec∪Fc⟹P((E∩F)c)=P(Ec∪Fc)

(E∪F)c=Ec∩Fc⟹P((E∪F)c)=P(Ec∩Fc)

2.1 Complement identities derived from De Morgan

Using P(A)+P(Ac)=1 together with De Morgan’s Law:

P(E∩F)​=1−P((E∩F)c)=1−P(Ec∪Fc)\[4pt]P(E∪F)​=1−P((E∪F)c)=1−P(Ec∩Fc)​

These are extremely useful whenever a question gives you complement-side information and asks for a union or intersection.

3. Worked PYQs

GATE CSE 1997 — Rain Today and Tomorrow Question: The probability that it will rain today is 0.5. The probability that it will rain tomorrow is 0.6. The probability that it will rain either today or tomorrow is 0.7. What is the probability that it will rain today and tomorrow?

Approach: Step 1 — Recognize this is a direct Inclusion-Exclusion setup. Step 2 — Let x=P(today∩tomorrow).

P(today∪tomorrow)=P(today)+P(tomorrow)−x

0.7=0.5+0.6−x ⇒ x=1.1−0.7=0.4

Answer: 0.4​ (Option D)

GATE IT 2008 — Union from Complements Question: A sample space has two events A and B such that P(A∩B)=21​, P(A′)=31​, P(B′)=31​. Find P(A∪B).

Approach: Step 1 — Convert complements to direct probabilities: P(A)=1−31​=32​, P(B)=32​. Step 2 — Apply Inclusion-Exclusion.

P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)=32​+32​−21​=34​−21​=68−3​=65​

Answer: 10/12=5/6​ (Option B)
