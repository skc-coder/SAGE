* 0.2 Grammatical Basis of Language Translation

** Overview
Much of the early study of language translation focuses on problems related
to language processing. This section reviews key concepts and terminology
associated with the language translation process.

** Natural Languages vs. Formal Languages
A useful way to distinguish between *natural* and *formal* languages is
to ask:
- *Which came first: the language or its grammatical rules?*

*** Natural Languages
- Evolved over time for human communication.
- Examples: English, German, Russian.
- Continue to evolve without strict adherence to formal grammatical rules.
- Grammatical rules are typically developed *after the fact* to describe
  observed usage.
- Rarely conform to simple or consistent grammatical structures.
- Nonconformance frustrates grammarians, who may blame speakers rather
  than accept that:
  - Existing rules are incomplete or outdated, or
  - No fixed set of rules fully captures the language.

*** Formal Languages
- Defined explicitly by pre-established rules.
- Examples:
  - Programming languages
  - Mathematical languages (e.g., algebra, propositional logic)
- Rigorously conform to their grammatical definitions.
- This strict structure enables efficient computerized translation.
- In contrast, the lack of strict rules in natural languages makes
  automated translation significantly more difficult.

** The Role of Grammar in Language Translation
To understand the importance of grammar in translation, consider the steps
performed by a typical language translator, such as a compiler.

** Components of a Language Translator

*** Lexical Analysis
- The first major task of a translator.
- Identifies the basic building blocks of the language.
- Groups sequences of symbols into meaningful units.
- Examples:
  - `LIST` → a single identifier
  - `524` → a single number
  - `:=` → a single assignment operator
- Performed by the *lexical analyzer*.
- Input: a string of symbols (source program).
- Output: a sequence of *tokens*.
  - Each token represents a single logical object.
  - Tokens may be stored as compact values (e.g., integers).

*** Parsing
- Analyzes the structure and pattern of tokens.
- Determines how tokens fit together according to grammatical rules.
- Examples:
  - Recognizing `if–then–else` structures
  - Identifying loop bodies between `repeat` and `until`
- Performed by the *parser*.

*** Code Generation
- Invoked by the parser as structures are recognized.
- Produces the translated output for each program component.
- Gradually constructs the final translated program (object program).

** Summary of the Translation Process
The translation process consists of three core components:
1. Lexical analysis
2. Parsing
3. Code generation

These components rely heavily on pattern recognition.
- When patterns are governed by strict grammatical rules (formal languages),
  recognition is feasible and efficient.
- When a language does not adhere to well-defined grammar (natural languages),
  constructing a reliable parser becomes extremely difficult.

** Key Insight
- Formal grammatical rules enable systematic translation.
- The absence of such rules is the primary obstacle in translating natural
  languages using traditional compiler techniques.

* 0.4 Preview of the Remaining Text

** Purpose of This Section
This section concludes the preliminary chapter by providing an overview
of the topics covered in the remaining chapters of the text.

** Chapters 1–3: Classes of Abstract Machines
Chapters 1 through 3 focus on three increasingly powerful classes of
theoretical machines:

- Finite automata
- Pushdown automata
- Turing machines

Each class is strictly more powerful than the previous one.

*** Pattern Recognition Focus
- The computational power of each machine is studied in terms of the
  *symbol patterns* it can recognize.
- The central question:
  - What kinds of patterns can each class of machine recognize?

*** Relevance to Language Processing
- Pattern recognition is fundamental to language processing systems.
- Compilers for modern high-level programming languages rely heavily on
  pattern-recognition techniques.
- Therefore, the theory developed in these chapters forms the foundation
  of modern compiler construction.
- The relationship between theory and practice will be explored throughout
  these chapters.

** Connection to Linguistics
- The material in Chapters 1–3 also applies to natural language processing.
- The hierarchy of machines corresponds to a hierarchy of languages
  discovered in the 1950s by linguist Noam Chomsky.
- Chomsky’s work aimed to establish a formal foundation for linguistic
  studies.
- As a result, the study of pattern-recognition machines is closely related
  to the study of linguistics.

** Limits of Computational Power
- The study of abstract machines leads to an apparent upper bound on
  computational ability.
- No class of machines—actual or theoretical—has been shown to be more
  powerful than Turing machines.
- This observation leads to *Turing’s Thesis*:
  - Turing machines can solve any problem that is solvable by computational
    means.
- The study of computability is framed within the context of this thesis.

** Chapter 4: Evidence for Turing’s Thesis
Chapter 4 examines evidence supporting Turing’s Thesis by comparing it
with results from other areas of computation theory.

*** Recursive Function Theory
- Introduces recursive function theory.
- Identifies an apparent computational bound known as *Church’s Thesis*.
- Shows that Church’s Thesis is equivalent to Turing’s Thesis.

*** Additional Supporting Evidence
- Examines computational limits in programming language design.
- Once again, these limits align with the computational power of Turing
  machines.

** Chapter 5: Practical Computability
Chapter 5 approaches computability from a practical perspective rather
than a purely theoretical one.

*** Resource Constraints
- Discusses challenges in classifying problems based on:
  - Time requirements
  - Storage (space) requirements
- Many problems:
  - Are theoretically solvable
  - Require impractically large amounts of time or memory
  - Therefore remain unsolvable in practice

*** Open Problems
- Some problems resist classification.
- It remains unknown whether practical algorithmic solutions exist for
  these problems.

** Overall Perspective
The remaining chapters cover a broad range of topics, including:

- Abstract concepts:
  - Computational power of theoretical machines
  - Ultimate limits of computation
- Practical concerns:
  - Compiler construction techniques
  - Computational power of programming languages
  - Feasibility of solving problems using computers

Together, these topics connect theoretical foundations with real-world
applications.

* Chapter 1: Finite Automata and Regular Languages

** Chapter Overview
This chapter introduces and studies a class of theoretical machines known
as *finite automata*.

- Finite automata have limited computational power.
- Despite these limitations, they are capable of recognizing many useful
  symbol patterns.
- The collection of symbol patterns recognizable by finite automata is
  called the class of *regular languages*.

** Position in the Machine Hierarchy
- Finite automata and regular languages form the *lowest level* of the
  hierarchy of machines and languages studied in the next three chapters.
- Higher levels in the hierarchy build upon the concepts introduced here.

** Theoretical Importance
- As the foundational level of the hierarchy, finite automata play a key
  role in understanding:
  - The nature of computation
  - The progression from simple to more powerful computational models

** Practical Significance
- The importance of finite automata and regular languages extends beyond
  theoretical computer science.
- The concepts introduced in this chapter are fundamental to applications
  involving pattern recognition.
- Common application areas include:
  - Language processing
  - Compiler construction
  - Text searching and matching
  - Lexical analysis

** Key Insight
- Even computationally simple machines can recognize rich and useful
  classes of patterns.
- Understanding finite automata provides a foundation for both theoretical
  study and practical pattern-recognition techniques.
